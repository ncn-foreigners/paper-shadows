\begin{abstract}
We estimate the unauthorised foreign population using aggregated administrative data on detected migrants, the authorised population, and
enforcement intensity. A general community-anchored modelling framework is proposed, which allows covariate-varying power-law relationships between authorised and unauthorised stocks to be estimated under suitable specifications. Analytical and bootstrap confidence intervals are available for inference. Application to Poland (2019--2024) puts the estimated adult non-Schengen unauthorised population size at about 98,000 (80\% interval: 38,000--227,000) in 2019 and 108,000 (80\% interval: 48,000--188,000) in 2024. While the authorised reference population almost doubled over this period (from 612,000 to 1,134,000), the estimated unauthorised population stayed broadly flat, so its share fell from 16.0\% to 9.5\%; the trends diverge by origin, with the Ukrainian component declining from 63,000 to 12,000 after the 2022 Temporary Protection Directive while non-Ukrainians rise from 35,000 to 96,000. The modelling frame work and application template are transferable to other countries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

In 2025, YouGov conducted a~survey in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Denmark, Poland, and the UK, finding that support for reducing
immigration is high and that these sentiments may be influenced by misperceptions about the scale of irregular migration. For example, in
Poland, 36\% believed that there are more illegal than legal migrants, compared to 28\% who think the opposite, and 22\% who believe the
numbers are roughly equal \citep{yougov2025immigration}. At the same time, close to 5 million migrants were registered in the Polish
population register (PESEL) at the end of 2025, while those detected by Polish authorities as staying illegally numbered only about 10,000,
though this figure captures only detected cases, not the total unauthorised population.

This type of mistaken belief is not new and is driven by various factors \citep[cf.][]{alesina2023immigration, ajzenman2023immigration, lutz2023misperceptions}. Given that misperceptions stem from being misinformed and uninformed, plausible estimates of the hidden population size are essential for designing public policies, allocating public funding, and planning public services. 

The size of the native-born population in a~given country may be reasonably well-known  at the national level, although the methods vary greatly across countries due to differences in statistical infrastructure: presence or absence of population registers (as in the UK and USA), variation in register quality, reliance on large-scale sample surveys (such as the American Community Survey; ACS) or population censuses. See e.g. \citet{skinner2018issues} or \citet{dunne2024system}.

Foreign nationals can be generally divided into two main groups: those who stay or reside legally with valid permits, and those who are unauthorised---staying or residing without valid documentation. The latter group, in particular, is widely recognised as hard-to-reach and hard-to-measure
\citep{mckenzie2009surveying,mendez2013surveying}, including the countries that have high-quality population registers \citep{danko2024assessing,monti2020over, careja2018using}, as by definition these people are excluded from the population register or other important registers, and also have incentives to avoid detection. In this paper, we focus on the unauthorised population.

Poland provides a~case-in-point that illustrates the estimation challenge. First, between 2015 and 2024, the registered foreign nationals increased from 200,000 to nearly 4.5 million\footnote{These figures are based on the popilation (PESEL hereinafter) register and indicate the number of people who registered for a~PESEL identifier, not how many actually reside in Poland. We discuss other registers later in the paper.}. Second, between 2018 and 2023, Poland issued over 6 million visas\footnote{This number refers to all visas type: includes short-term visas, renewals, and all consular visa issuances. For more details see \citet{nik2024fullreport_en}.} (about 3.8 million of which were work visas), placing it among the EU countries issuing the most visas of this type. Third, following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, over 3.5 million refugees transited through Poland between February and mid-May 2022, with about 1 million remaining under temporary protection as of 31 December 2024 (cf. \cite{stats-poland-2024, duszczyk2022war, duszczyk2023war}, and \url{https://ukraincywpolsce.stat.gov.pl}).
Finally, from 2021, Russia and Belarus have placed pressure on the Belarusian-Polish-EU border through politically instrumentalised migration, influencing the scale of unauthorised entry, but with limited political implications \citep{Charasz2025}. This combination of rapid demographic change, high visa issuance, humanitarian flows, and border pressures creates conditions where unauthorised populations are likely substantial but hard to estimate.

In this paper, we propose a~general modelling framework for estimating the size of the unauthorised foreign population, building on the community-anchored model introduced by \citet{Zhang2008} for a~one-off application to Norwegian data. \citet{Zhang2008} established identifiability of the power-law relationship between authorised and unauthorised populations under Poisson log-linearity; we extend it into a~framework that accommodates data over multiple years and subpopulations, with covariate-varying parameters, robust inference of uncertainty, and estimator choice guided by simulation evidence. 

Unlike other existing approaches --- such as the residual method 
\citep[cf.][]{warren1987count, chassamboulli2015labor, van2021uncertainty},
capture-recapture or multiple systems estimation
\citep[cf.][]{chipperfield2024robust, bohning2024one, aleshin2024central},
and deductive or digital-trace methods \citep{recchi2026overstayers} --- which require linkage across multiple data sources or unit-level micro data at specific time points, our method requires only aggregated data. We develop a~model grounded in demographic and behavioural assumptions that uses three inputs: \emph{aggregated counts} of detected unauthorised migrants, the size of the authorised foreign nationals, and a~covariate proxying for detection intensity. 

Our key assumptions include: (1) there exists a~structural relationship between unauthorised and authorised foreign nationals due to the former's reliance on communities or networks for survival; (2) it is valid to target a~theoretical size of unauthorised population without spurious day-to-day variations; and (3) the detection rate of unauthorised foreigners can be modelled statistically as a~function of known features that proxy the relevant channels or mechanisms.

Support of such a~theoretical model can be found in two earlier EU-funded research programmes. The CLANDESTINO project
\citep{CLANDESTINO2009} documented the extent to which unauthorised migrants depend on established co-ethnic communities for housing,
employment, and social support --- the same network mechanism is captured by the community anchor parameter in our model. The MIrreM project \citep{kierans2025handbook} highlighted severe biases of sizing by raw apprehension data directly, which reflect enforcement priorities and capacity constraints rather than the underlying unauthorised population --- the insight is reflected in our detection intensity parameter which connects to the observed counts of unauthorised people to the unobserved population sizes. Our modelling framework thus answers a~call form both these projects for methods that move beyond the contested administrative counts toward credible population estimates.
 
Applied to the Polish data from 2019 to 2024, our approach yields plausible trajectory of estimates despite the structural shock caused by the Ukraine war.  Our estimate of the adult (18+) non-Schengen unauthorised population size in Poland stands at about 98,000 (80\% interval: 38,000--227,000) in 2019 and 108,000 (80\% interval: 48,000--188,000) in 2024.\footnote{In the abstract and introduction we report 80\% intervals; the tables report both 80\% and 95\% intervals. Because the bootstrap distribution is right-skewed, the interval bounds indicate estimation uncertainty rather than equally plausible population sizes, and the central (median) estimate is the most probable value. Point estimates and interval bounds for unauthorised population sizes are rounded to the nearest 1,000; detailed tables retain finer precision.} A central message is one of relative decline: even as Poland's registered foreign population surged and the ZUS reference population almost doubled (from 612,000 to 1,134,000), the estimated unauthorised population remained broadly stable, so its share of the ZUS reference population fell from 16.0\% in 2019 to 9.5\% in 2024. This relative finding is robust to the considerable width of the underlying confidence intervals.

Moreover, our estimates capture divergent dynamics by the country of origin. The estimated Ukrainian unauthorised population declines from 63,000 in 2019 to 12,000 in 2024, consistent with the mass regularisation under the 2022 EU Temporary Protection Directive, while the non-Ukrainian component moves in the opposite direction, rising from 35,000 to 96,000 over the same period. 

Sensitivity analyses suggest that these estimates are robust against model mis-specifications that are unavoidable, and aligns plausibly with other administrative data used in the estimation. In particular, the wide confidence intervals reflect the inherent uncertainty in estimating hidden populations, and it is important for users and policy makers to pay attention to the range of plausible values rather than relying too much on any single point estimate.

Finally, our approach is easily transferable to other countries, since it relies only on aggregated administrative data that exist commonly, and its efficacy has already been demonstrated in quite different situations from Norway to Poland. The relevant methodology has the potential for National Statistical Offices to improve migration estimates by adding the \emph{uncounted} component.

In the rest of the paper, Section~\ref{sec-theory} presents the theoretical model, Section~\ref{sec-empirical} the empirical specification, 
Sections~\ref{sec-simulation}--\ref{sec-results} describe the application to Polish data, and the conclusions are drawn in Section~\ref{sec-conclusions}. Additional details are given in the Supplementary Materials.


\section{Theoretical model}\label{sec-theory}

To formalise our theoretical model, let $M_{it}$ be the size of the unauthorised population of \emph{community} $i$ $(i=1,\ldots,I)$ in period $t$ $(t=1,\ldots,T)$, and let $N_{it}$ denote the size of a~corresponding authorised \emph{reference population}. Let $m_{it}$ be the \emph{detected} count of unauthorised migrants (among the $M_{it}$), and let $n_{it}$ denote a~known count which may or may not correspond to a~subset among the $N_{it}$, such that $n_{it}/N_{it}$ provides a~feature for \emph{detection-intensity} related to $m_{it}/M_{it}$.

We define the \emph{theoretical} size of unauthorised population as the conditional expectation of $M_{it}$‚
\begin{equation}
\xi_{it} = \mathbb{E}(M_{it} \mid N_{it}).
\label{eq-xi-expectation}
\end{equation} 
Let the conditional expectation of the \emph{detection rate} $p_{it} = m_{it}/M_{it}$ given the others be 
\begin{equation}
\rho_{it} = \mathbb{E}(p_{it} \mid M_{it}, N_{it}, n_{it}).
\label{eq-rho-expectation}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq-xi-expectation} and \eqref{eq-rho-expectation} under the assumptions (i) $M_{it}$ and $p_{it}$ are conditionally independent given $(N_{it}, n_{it})$, and (ii) $n_{it}$ provides no additional information about $M_{it}$ beyond $N_{it}$, we obtain the expectation of the detected count $m_{it} = M_{it} p_{it}$ as 
\begin{equation}
\mu_{it} = \mathbb{E}\{ M_{it}\cdot \mathbb{E}(p_{it} \mid M_{it}) \mid N_{it}, n_{it}\} 
= \mathbb{E}(M_{it} \mid N_{it}) \cdot \mathbb{E}(p_{it} \mid N_{it},n_{it}) = \xi_{it} \cdot \rho_{it}
\label{eq-counts-expectation}
\end{equation}
The functional forms of $\xi_{it}$ and $\rho_{it}$, as well as the distributional assumptions of $m_{it}$, will be treated later as part of our empirical model in Section \ref{sec-empirical}. Below we discuss first the motivation and justification of the theoretical model defined by \eqref{eq-xi-expectation}--\eqref{eq-counts-expectation}.

First, our core assumption is that the size of the unauthorised population is structurally related to the authorised population via communities, which typically concur with the country of origin, ethnicity, or similar characteristics. This is supported by migration research showing that unauthorised migrants depend heavily on co-ethnic and kin-based networks for survival and incorporation into host societies
\citep{Hagan1998, Engbersen2000, Ambrosini2023}. Such networks serve multiple functions: providing accommodation, information, informal
employment, and emotional support, and often substitute for formal welfare and labour market institutions that unauthorised migrants cannot
access \citep{Anderson2007, McDowell2009}. Consequently, we expect the unauthorised population from a~given origin country to scale with the
corresponding authorised population, which serves as the foundation for these support networks.

Moreover, unlike the residual or capture-recapture methods, which treat the unauthorised population size as an unknown constant, we view $M_{it}$ as a~random variable arsing from processes driven by economic, political, and demographic factors, and model its theoretical size $\xi_{it}$ in relation to a~suitable reference population size $N_{it}$, i.e. \eqref{eq-xi-expectation}. This allows us to capture how the two populations co-evolve: as authorised communities grow, they provide expanded networks that support larger unauthorised populations from the same origin or ethnicity. Conditioning on $N_{it}$ also makes it possible to interpret the estimate of $\xi_{it}$ without spurious day-to-day variations, just like how standard official population statistics are received.   

Regarding the detection rate $p_{it}$ that connects the observed count $m_{it}$ and the unobserved population size $M_{it}$, the factorisation in \eqref{eq-counts-expectation} requires that $\mathbb{E}(p_{it} \mid M_{it}, N_{it}, n_{it}) = \mathbb{E}(p_{it} \mid N_{it}, n_{it})$,
meaning the detection rate does not depend on the size of the unauthorised population. This assumption rules out feedback
effects whereby detection intensity $\rho_{it}$ responds endogenously to $M_{it}$. Although it cannot be tested directly since $M_{it}$ is unobserved, the data of staffing and enforcement activities in Poland (see Table~\ref{tbl-appen-border-guard} in Supplementary Materials) show that legality-of-stay inspections fluctuated over 2019--2024 without a~systematic trend, Police and Border Guard staffing remained broadly stable despite substantial growth of the presence of foreign nationals, whereas employment legality inspections actually declined over the period. Altogether they suggest that detection intensity is shaped by supply-side factors (budget cycles, staffing constraints, pandemic disruptions) rather than by endogenous responses to $M_{it}$, rendering the no-feedback assumption empirically plausible.

Of course, we must caution against taking this for granted. For example, congestion effects may arise when enforcement resources are fixed relative to the unauthorised population, causing the detection rate to decline as $M_{it}$ increases \citep{fender1999general, hanson1999illegal}. Conversely, concentration effects may cause detection rate to increase with $M_{it}$ if larger populations are more visible to enforcement \citep{ethier1986illegal}. However, without additional data or instrumental variables, it is infeasible to distinguish the variation in $p_{it}$ from that in $M_{it}$ when the former depends on the latter  \citep{link2003nonidentifiability}. In other words, different approaches will be necessary in such situations. 

Finally, by way of introduction to the empirical model, we notice the flexibility of this theoretical model. On the one hand, it allows one to estimate the total size of unauthorised population without decomposing the counts $m_{it}$ into visa overstayers, border apprehensions, and so on. Attempts to deal with these subgroups separately, originated from different data sources, may cause complications and aggregation inconsistencies \citep{warren1987count}. On the other hand, it is possible to disaggregate $m_{it}$ by relevant covariates such as gender and age categories, when it comes to the empirical model specification, provided this is sensible and supported by the available data. 



\section{Empirical model}\label{sec-empirical}

\subsection{Parametric specification}\label{parametric-specification}

As the first part of our empirical model, we now specify the functional forms for \eqref{eq-xi-expectation} and \eqref{eq-rho-expectation}. For the theoretical size of unauthorised population, a~power-law relationship is given as
\begin{equation}\label{eq-xi-form}
\xi_{it} = \mathbb{E}(M_{it} \mid N_{it}) = N_{it}^{\alpha_{it}},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{it} > 0$ is an unknown \emph{Community Anchor} parameter, capturing how the unauthorised population scales with the reference population for the given community and time. Let the corresponding detection intensity $\rho_{it}$ be given as
\begin{equation}\label{eq-rho-form}
\rho_{it} = \mathbb{E}(p_{it} \mid N_{it}, n_{it}) = \left( \gamma + \frac{n_{it}}{N_{it}}\right)^{\beta_{it}},
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{it} >0$ is an unknown parameter of \emph{Exposure Elasticity}, regulating how the detection intensity responds to the chosen feature  $n_{it} / N_{it}$, and $\gamma > 0$ represents a~\emph{(baseline) offset} even when $n_{it} = 0$. Notice that $\rho_{it} \in (0,1)$ as long as $0< \gamma + n_{it} / N_{it} <1$.

The community-anchored model of \citet{Zhang2008} is a~special case that uses the simplest parameterisation $(\alpha_{it}, \beta_{it}, \gamma) \equiv (\alpha, \beta, 0)$  for a~one-off application in Norway, where $t$ refers to a~single year 2006 and $i$ refers to the countries of citizenship. In particular, all the countries with $m_i n_i =0$ are \emph{regrouped} into a~`rest' community, so that $\rho_{it} >0$ everywhere even with $\gamma \equiv 0$. The parameterisation here in terms of \eqref{eq-xi-form} and \eqref{eq-rho-form} is more general and principled.

Firstly, to accommodate multiple periods and flexible formulation of communities, we allow $\alpha_{it}$ to vary with $(x_i, t)$, where $x_i$ denotes the relevant covariates of the communities. For example, let $x_i =1$ for males and $x_i = 2$ for females from country $i$. By suitable dummy-coding of $(x_i, t)$, we can allow distinct $\alpha$-values from crossing $x_i$ and $t$ directly, or as the sum of a~gender effect and a~year effect but without any interaction of the two. Similarly for $\beta_{it}$, with a~separate parameterisation, such as $\beta(t)$ and $\alpha(x_i, t)$ at the same time. 

Next, conceptually speaking, the detection intensity $\rho_{it}$ is never zero since, even when the feature count is $n_{it} = 0$, it is still possible to detect unauthorised migrants through various channels, such as border guards, hospital admissions, rejected visa or asylum applications, employer
inspections. Introducing the offset $\gamma$ is a~more principled approach than regrouping to form ad hoc `rest' communities, because the composition of such `rest' communities will not be the same over time. Moreover, it allows one to make better use of the data points with $m_{it} >0$ and $n_{it} =0$.


\subsection{Motivation and justification of the parametric specification}\label{sec-emp-just}

Various functional forms could relate $\xi_{it}$ to $N_{it}$. Our choice of the power-law specification \eqref{eq-xi-form} is consistent with the 
considerations underlying the theoretical assumption \eqref{eq-xi-expectation}. Unauthorised migrants lack access to regular employment and welfare services, requiring a~network of contacts to survive \citep{Hagan1998, Engbersen2000}. These contacts most likely include usual residents in established socio-economic situations, which provides the ``existence basis'' for the unauthorised population, analogous to how a~reproductive
population supports offspring in demographic models.

It is therefore natural to expect a~relationship where $\xi_{it}$ increases with $N_{it}$, but the rate of increase declines as $N_{it}$ becomes
larger\footnote{\citet{massey1990social} introduced the idea of
  cumulative causation in migration, emphasising how migrant networks
  create self-reinforcing migration processes. \citet{fussell2004limits}
  show that these mechanisms are much weaker in large urban settings
  than in smaller communities. \citet{carrington1996migration} provide a
  model in which moving costs decline as migrant networks expand,
  generating gradual self-reinforcing migration dynamics.}. In particular, our
specification closely relates to the \cite{beverton_holt_1957} model, which is a~discrete-time population model given by
\[
R = \frac{\nu S}{1 + \kappa S},
\]
where $S$ is the current population size, $R$ is the next-generation population, $\kappa$ is the density dependence parameter, and $\nu/\kappa$ is the carrying capacity. The power curve $N^{\alpha}$ and the Beverton-Holt curve exhibit similar density-dependent behaviour, particularly over empirically relevant ranges of $N$. The key difference is that the Beverton-Holt model assumes an asymptotic ceiling $\nu/\kappa$, whereas the power-law specification allows $\xi_{it} \to \infty$ as $N_{it} \to \infty$. An absolute ceiling of the unauthorised population size is not necessary in our context.

In contrast, the power-law specification \eqref{eq-rho-form} does not suggest a~direct connection from the chosen feature rate $n_{it}/N_{it}$ to the detection rate $m_{it}/M_{it}$ of unauthorised migrants. The count $n_{it}$ should be chosen such that the corresponding rate $n_{it}/N_{it}$ may proxy for detection intensity through various mechanisms, the elasticity of which can be tuned by the parameter $\beta_{it}$. For instance, if $n_{it}$ is based on police records of foreign nationals facing criminal charges, there is no direct connection between criminal behaviour and unauthorised status---rather, contact with law enforcement creates opportunities for detection regardless of criminality. Similarly, when $n_{it}$ is derived from events recorded in administrative systems, it may proxy the degree to which migrants interact with the relevant institutions or agencies that may detect unauthorised foreigners (e.g., hospital admissions, rejections of visa/asylum applications from the previous year).

We notice that it is possible to adopt logistic functions to ensure $\alpha_{it} <1$ or $\rho_{it} <1$; see Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-ommited-proofs}. However, log transformation corresponding to the power-specification is preferable because it enables us to combine equations \eqref{eq-xi-form} and \eqref{eq-rho-form} directly on the log scale, as long as the restrictions are empirically unnecessary for the data at hand.

We obtain straightforward model diagnostics as one of the benefits. Plugging in $m_{it}$ for $\mu_{it} = \xi_{it} \rho_{it}$ given by \eqref{eq-xi-form} and \eqref{eq-rho-form}, dividing both sides by $N_{it}$ and taking logarithms for the data points with $n_{it} > 0$ yields, for each period $t$ with $(\alpha_{it}, \beta_{it}, \gamma) = (\alpha_t, \beta_t, 0)$, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq-diagnostic-model}
\log\left(\frac{m_{it}}{N_{it}}\right) = (\alpha_t - 1) \log N_{it} + \beta_t \log\left(\frac{n_{it}}{N_{it}}\right).
\end{equation}
Plotting $\log(m_{it}/N_{it})$ against each covariate on the right side provides a~diagnostic check: by the considerations above, we expect $\alpha_t - 1 < 0$, i.e., a~negative relationship with $\log N_{it}$, and $\beta_t > 0$, i.e., a~positive relationship with $\log(n_{it}/N_{it})$. Furthermore, stratifying these plots by country groups (e.g., by continent or migration corridor) allows us to assess whether a~single pattern holds uniformly or systematic differences exist to warrant more disaggregated parameterisation. Similarly with other partitions of all the data points for exploratory data analysis. 

Finally, although the model can be tested empirically for goodness-of-fit to observed counts $m_{it}$, the specifications \eqref{eq-xi-form} and \eqref{eq-rho-form} cannot be separately verified. In other words, it is logically possible for other empirical models of $\mu_{it}$ to fit equally well to the observed counts $m_{it}$, such that a~choice must rely on certain considerations that cannot be directly tested. This is a~fundamental limitation inherent to estimating hidden populations, and we would caution against overconfidence in the results. Nevertheless, we have not found decisive evidence against specifications \eqref{eq-xi-form} and \eqref{eq-rho-form} in our applications, and the motivations outlined above provide a~coherent rationale.


\subsection{Parameter estimation}\label{estimation}
 
To estimate the mean structure \eqref{eq-xi-form}--\eqref{eq-rho-form}, it suffices to assume independent Poisson distributions of $m_{it}$, denoted by  
\begin{equation}\label{eq-poisson-model}
m_{it} \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_{it}), \quad \log(\mu_{it}) = \alpha_{it}(\eta) \log N_{it} + \beta_{it}(\eta) \log\left(\gamma + \frac{n_{it}}{N_{it}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ contains all the unknown constants in the specifications of $(\alpha_{it}, \beta_{it})$, and $\Pr(m_{it}=0) >0$ exists by the Poisson distribution such that all the data points can be used in the estimation directly. However, since the variance structure of $m_{it}$ is most likely misspecified in this way, we shall refer to the resulting estimator as a~pseudo maximum likelihood estimator (PMLE), instead of MLE given the Poisson distribution. The Poisson PMLE would coincide with the MLE when the Poisson distribution holds, but it requires only correct mean specification for consistency regardless of the true variance structure \citep{SantosSilvaTenreyro2006}. 

Notice that \citet{Zhang2008} accommodates over-dispersion of $m_{it}$ by explicitly assuming 
\[
m_{it} \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_{it} u_{it}) \quad\text{and}\quad u_{it} \sim \text{Gamma}(1, \phi),
\]
where $\text{Gamma}(1, \phi)$ denotes the gamma distribution with expectation 1 and variance $1/\phi$. The Poisson-Gamma mixture yields the Negative Binomial (NB) likelihood on integrating out the unobserved random effects $u_{it}$, and the corresponding NB-MLE. In Norway applications, the NB-MLE was obtained with $\gamma \equiv 0$ in \eqref{eq-rho-form} and regrouping of data points to avoid $m_i n_i =0$.  

Given non-trivial offset $\gamma >0$, the NB-MLE is feasible without regrouping data points. Due to its ability to accommodate over-dispersion of $m_{it}$, which exists commonly in real data, the formal model-selection criteria such as AIC or BIC tend to favour NB-MLE to Poisson PMLE. However, as we explain below, there are several reasons for preferring the Poisson PMLE to NB-MLE in practice, specially when non-trivial offset $\gamma >0$ is allowed in \eqref{eq-rho-form}. 

\emph{Offset-Gamma confounding.} A manifestation of over-dispersion in the present context is the data points with $m_{it} >0$ but $n_{it} =0$. This can be partly dealt with by the non-trivial offset $\gamma$, and partly by the random effects $u_{it}$ in the Poisson-Gamma mixture. This confounding would affect the estimated mean structure through $\gamma$. As we document in Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-cov-gamma}, the profile log-likelihood of $\gamma$ can be rather flat for the NB-MLE, whilst it peaks sharply for the Poisson PMLE in comparison. More on this in the simulation study in Section \ref{sec-simulation}.

\emph{Small-counts sensitivity.} The ability of NB-MLE to accommodate over-dispersion carries a~drawback as it becomes sensitive to the effects of small counts $m_{it}$. See the relevant measures in Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-diagnostics}, and the consequences on population size estimation. When NB-MLE is applied with $t$-specific parameter \eqref{eq-xi-form}, it can lead to implausible spikes and disruptions of the estimated population size over time, compared to the Poisson PMLE. Notice that, since the extent of small counts is much reduced when `rest' communities are created from the data points with $m_{it} n_{it} =0$, this problem is somewhat related to offset-Gamma confounding above. 

\emph{Robustness of PMLE.} The Poisson PMLE requires only correct specification of the mean of $m_{it}$ for consistency \citep{SantosSilvaTenreyro2006}; however, see Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-sim-study} and Section~\ref{sec-simulation} for finite-sample caveats. A useful indication of robust mean estimation by Poisson PMLE is whether the associated confidence interval includes the NB-MLE. Notice that, in practice, one may find the trajectory of unauthorised population size estimates to be more reliable by Poisson PMLE than NB-MLE, even when the former is not more `correct' for each given period.   

 
\subsection{Estimation of unauthorised population size}\label{estimating-the-unauthorised-population-size-and-its-variance}

Given the estimates $\hat{\alpha}_{it} = \alpha_{it}(\hat{\eta})$ from \eqref{eq-poisson-model}, we can estimate the overall size of unauthorised population for a~period $t$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq-estimators}
\hat{\xi}_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{I} N_{it}^{\hat{\alpha}_{it}} 
\end{equation}
Consistency of $\hat{\xi}_t$ follows from the Continuous Mapping Theorem applied to $g(\hat{\eta})$. Meanwhile, in finite-sample applications, one may consider to reduce the potential bias due to the non-linear transformation involved, which would result in an estimator given as  
\begin{equation}\label{eq-bias-correction}
\hat{\xi}_t^{\text{BC}} = \sum_{i=1}^{I} N_{it}^{\hat{\alpha}_{it}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (\log N_{it})^2 \sigma_{it}^2 \right),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{it}^2$ is a~term related to the variance of $\hat{\eta}$ --- see Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-ommited-proofs} for the full derivation and Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-sim-study} for finite-sample simulation results. However, the adjustment is typically small compared to the width of confidence interval.  

A confidence interval of $\alpha_{it}$, denoted by $(\hat{\alpha}_{it}^{\text{lower}}, \hat{\alpha}_{it}^{\text{upper}})$, can be obtained using heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) standard errors  \citep{mackinnon1985some}. Since $g(\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{I} N_{it}^{\alpha}$ is monotonically increasing in $\alpha$ given $N_{it} \geq 1$, a~confidence level of $\xi_t$ is given directly as
\begin{equation}\label{eq-xi-confint}
\left( \sum_{i=1}^{I} N_{it}^{\hat{\alpha}_{it}^{\text{lower}}}, \;
\sum_{i=1}^{I} N_{it}^{\hat{\alpha}_{it}^{\text{upper}}} \right).
\end{equation}
However, in practice, one may still encounter situations where the interval can be excessively wide due to ill-behaving $(\hat{\alpha}_{it}^{\text{lower}}, \hat{\alpha}_{it}^{\text{upper}})$ for a~subset of all $\alpha_{it}$.

We therefore prefer the fractional weighted bootstrap {[}FWB; \citet{XuEtAl2020}{]} with clustering by country-of-origin.
The approach simultaneously accounts for within-country correlation across years and covariates, the nonlinear transformation of parameter estimates, and their skewed distributions. In particular, one may consider the bootstrap median of $\{\hat{\xi}_t\}$ as the point estimate, which is more robust to skewness than either a~plug-in estimator or the bootstrap mean.

It is worth noting that, in applications, wide confidence intervals are a~well-documented feature of unauthorised population estimation, reflecting the inherent uncertainty in estimating hidden populations. This has been discussed extensively in the migration literature, including \citet{van2021uncertainty}, the Centre on
Migration, Policy and Society (COMPAS) estimates at the University of Oxford\footnote{See
  \url{https://migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk/resources/briefings/unauthorised-migration-in-the-uk/}}, the CLANDESTINO project \citep[cf.][]{CLANDESTINO2009}, and the MIrreM project \citep{kierans2025handbook}.



\section{Simulation study}\label{sec-simulation}

We assess the properties of estimation methods by simulations motivated from the 2024 Polish data. The main objective is to choose the suitable estimator for the application later. 

For each replication, we generate $(N_i, n_i, m_i)$ independently across the communities, where the number of communities is fixed at 100. First, let $N_i \sim \text{LogNormal}(5.5, 2.75)$, truncated to $N_i \geq 3$. Next, generate $n_i$ from either the Poisson or NB distribution, where $\log \mathbb{E}(n_i \mid N_i) = c_0 + c_1 \log N_i$, and the parameters of either distribution are derived from the police data $n_i$ and the ZUS register $N_i$ --- see Section \ref{auxiliary-detection-covariate} for details of these data sources. Finally, the mean of $m_i$ is given by \eqref{eq-xi-form} and \eqref{eq-rho-form} with $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = (0.7, 0.5, 0.005)$, and $m_i$ follows either the Poisson or NB distribution, derived from the Polish data --- see Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appendix-sim-study} for the details. Notice that the dispersion of $m_i$ under repeated simulation of $(N_i, n_i, m_i)$ in this way is actually larger than that observed in the 2024 Polish data, because $(N_i,n_i)$ are not constants in the simulation.
 
Crossing the two data-generating processes of $n_i$ and $m_i$ yields four scenarios: 
Po($n$)--Po($m$), Po($n$)--NB($m$), NB($n$)--Po($m$), NB($n$)--NB($m$).  
In addition, we consider two estimation setups. 
\begin{itemize} 
\item In Setup-I, we apply regrouping to create an ad hoc `rest' community from all the data points with $m_i n_i =0$, such that the detection intensity is modelled without the offset, i.e. $\gamma \equiv 0$ in \eqref{eq-rho-form}. Notice that the mean structure of Poisson PMLE or NB-MLE is misspecified in this setup, since all the data are generated with $\gamma =0.005$. 
\item In Setup-II, we allow for non-trivial offset $\gamma >0$ in \eqref{eq-rho-form}, such that the communities with $m_i n_i =0$ are used directly in estimation. Notice that the mean structure of Poisson PMLE or NB-MLE is correct in this setup, but neither the Poisson nor the NB likelihood is correct in any scenario since $(N_i,n_i)$ are not constants in the simulation.
\end{itemize}

Consider the Poisson PMLE. First, given Po($n$)--Po($m$), it is essentially unbiased in Setup-II and has the correct coverage at the 95\% level; although the mean structure is misspecified in Setup-I, it causes little bias given an offset of the magnitude $\gamma =0.005$, such that the larger RMSE in Setup-I is rather due to the loss of efficiency from regrouping to create the `rest' community. 

Next, given NB($n$)--NB($m$), Poisson PMLE does suffer from bias as well as a~very large variance (either in terms of the CI width or RMSE). This serves as a~warning that, despite the asymptotic consistency, the performance of Poisson PMLE can deteriorate greatly given small sample size, such as 100 here, and excessive over-dispersion, such as by LogN($N$)-NB($n$)--NB($m$). 

Consider now the NB-MLE. First, given Po($n$)--Po($m$), it is biased despite the correct mean structure in Setup-II: in fact, the low coverage of 77.4\% suggests that the bias is relatively large compared to the standard error. It suffers a~loss of efficiency in Setup-I due to regrouping, just like Poisson PMLE. Meanwhile, its bias is somewhat reduced from -3.9\% in Setup-II to -2.1\% Setup-I, which suggests that the sensitivity of estimating $\gamma$ (in Setup-II) can possibly be more damaging to NB-MLE than mean-mis-specification (in Setup-I) which avoids the need to estimate $\gamma$.  

Next, given NB($n$)–NB($m$), NB-MLE also suffers from bias but with a much smaller variance than Poisson PMLE; its bias is smaller in Setup-II (10.9\% vs -31.1\%) yet still large enough to lower the coverage, reflecting the sensitivity of estimating $\gamma$. This sensitivity is most severe when the data carry no over-dispersion: under Po(n)–Po(m), NB-MLE overestimates $\gamma = 0.005$ by a~factor of 24 ($\hat{\gamma}$ averages 0.12, hitting the upper boundary in 16\% of replications).

In summary, these results point to the following choices for the application to the Polish data: (i) allow offset $\gamma >0$ to avoid ad hoc regrouping of data over time, and the associated loss of efficiency; (ii) adopt Poisson PMLE as the primary estimator, but be aware of any excessive over-dispersion; (iii) NB-MLE is unstable due to offset-Gamma confounding and small-counts sensitivity, although it can still provide sensitivity checks with or without non-trivial offset $\gamma$, i.e. whether the NB-MLE is included in the confidence intervals based on the Poisson PMLE.

\section{Data sources} \label{sec-data}


\subsection{Definition of an unauthorised
stay}\label{definition-of-an-unauthorised-stay}

In our study, we use Polish Border Guard reports, which distinguish
three groups of migrants: (1) refusals of entry at the border, (2)
detections during irregular border crossings, and (3) detections for
illegal stay within the country or at the border when leaving. We focus
on the third group, and in particular those who were identified
\emph{within the country}. Groups 1 and 2 capture border-level
encounters (attempted or actual irregular crossings), whereas group 3
reflects detections of unauthorised residents already present on Polish
territory, which corresponds to the target population $M_{it}$ in our
theoretical model. Within group 3, we exclude those detected at the
border upon departure, as they are in the process of leaving the
unauthorised population and their inclusion could conflate exit flows
with the resident stock. Detailed definitions of each group are provided
in Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appen-definitions}.

Under Polish law, an unauthorised stay encompasses any presence that
violates entry or residence regulations---ranging from expired visas and
overstayed permits to illegal employment and Schengen Information System 
flagged individuals.
Two authorities verify legality of stay: the Border Guard (at the border
and within the country) and the Police. Note that a~person who crossed
the border illegally but was subsequently detected within the country
falls into our analysis sample under group 3. For the full legal
definition, consequences, and recent legislative changes, see
Supplementary Materials Section~\ref{sec-appen-definitions} and
Section~\ref{sec-appen-changes-law}. For a~comprehensive overview of the
forms and drivers of migrant irregularity in Poland---including visa
overstaying, fictitious employment declarations, and expired
pandemic-era permits---see \citet{yeliseyeu2024poland}. Finally, The Police record foreigners mainly in pre-trial criminal proceedings, which cover offences prosecuted
\emph{ex officio} and not unauthorised stay, a~petty offence handled by
the Border Guard; the two sources therefore overlap only marginally (see \ref{sec-appen-police}).

\subsection{Data sources}\label{data-sources}

\subsubsection{Observed unauthorised
population}\label{observed-unauthorised-population}

The first dataset, on the number of unauthorised migrants detected for
illegal stay, was prepared by representatives of the Polish Border
Guards on a~half-year basis with information on people who were
identified multiple times, along with their country of citizenship, sex
and age group.

The choice of reference population counts $N$ affects primarily the community-anchor parameter $\alpha$ and secondarily the exposure-elasticity parameter $\beta$. We decided to use the ZUS (Social Insurance Institution) register as the source of reference population counts $N$ for several reasons. First,

foreign nationals. Second, it contains records of foreign individuals
registered for social insurance by their employers, which provides a~strong signal of actual presence; since registration implies active

indeed residing, working or insured in Poland. In contrast, the Tax
register is updated only annually, limiting its temporal resolution.
Furthermore, the PESEL register suffers from well-documented
deregistration issues, where individuals who have left the country may
remain in the database, leading to overestimation of the resident
population.

Although NB-MLE with offset has been rejected as the primary estimator for this application, their results are included here to illustrate that formal model-selection criteria such as AIC and BIC do not handle random-effects models in the same way as fixed-effects models. In particular, the ability to accommodate over-dispersion may unduly favour random-effects models. A related problem is the much narrower range of AIC (or BIC) values as the specification of $\alpha_{it}$ varies, which shows that less information about the mean structure is captured by these criteria. 

When it comes to Poisson PMLE with offset, which is our focus here, there is a~large drop of AIC (or BIC) from yearly $\alpha$ (as in exploratory estimation above) to our preferred S8 specification. On the one hand, the interaction between year and Ukraine (separated from the other countries of origin) is the most important reason, as can be seen by comparing S8 to S4 specification. On the other hand, as described in Section \ref{sec-data}, sex is a~factor by which all the counts vary; the small change of AIC (or BIC) from S4 to S8 is simply due to the overwhelming mass of males in these data. 

The year-by-Ukrainian interaction in the community anchor is motivated by the 2022 EU Temporary Protection Directive, which expanded the regularised 
Ukrainian population $N$ without a~similar effect on the unauthorised counts $m$. Without this interaction, the community anchor would have to absorb this structural break as a~declining trend that affects the non-Ukrainians as well. 

In addition to the S8 specification of $\alpha_{it}$, with yearly $\beta_t$ and constant $\gamma$, we have also considered other specifications of $\beta$ and $\gamma$. Regarding the exposure elasticity $\beta_{it}$, adding sex or Ukrainian origin to yearly $\beta_t$ does not improve AIC/BIC and introduces instability, whereas holding $\beta$ constant across years would produce nearly identical population size trajectories by Poisson PMLE and NB-MLE, and misses the structural break noted above. Regarding the offset $\gamma$, allowing it to vary by covariates or time has a~larger effect on Poisson PMLE than NB-MLE, in the sense that AIC/BIC differs more for the former than the latter, which is similar to the contrast between them in Table \ref{tbl-aic-specs}, although the resulting population size estimates still fall within the confidence intervals by the S8 specification. In short, the S8 specification here balances responsiveness to structural break with estimation stability and model parsimony.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec-conclusions}

This paper proposes a~general community-anchored modelling framework for estimating the size of unauthorised populations using only aggregated
administrative data. The method requires three inputs: counts of detected unauthorised migrants, the size of an authorised foreign population (as a~reference), and a~covariate proxying detection intensity. 

The framework is applied to the Polish data from 2019 to 2024. Our primary specification allows the community anchor ($\alpha$) to vary by year, Ukrainian origin (with interaction), and sex, while the exposure elasticity ($\beta$) varies by year only, and the offset ($\gamma$) of the detection intensity is constant throughout. In particular, our reported estimate of the adult non-Schengen unauthorised population size is 108,000 (80\% interval: 48,000--188,000; 95\% CI: 32,000--248,000) in 2024, which stands at 9.5\% compared with the ZUS reference population. Males constitute the majority of the authorised population. The reported estimates are robust against potential model mis-specification, not least when one takes into account their associated confidence intervals. 


The 2022 EU Temporary Protection Directive massively expanded Ukraine's reference population without a~proportional increase in unauthorised Ukrainians. Our model captures this structural break naturally in terms of the year-by-Ukrainian interaction in the mean specification. This results in plausible as well as stable trajectory of the target population size over the entire period from 2019 to 2024. The framework is applicable to other large-scale regularisation or protection programmes that selectively inflates the lawful reference populations for specific communities. 

Several limitations should be noted for practitioners. First, the wide confidence intervals reflect the inherent uncertainty in estimating hidden or hard-to-reach populations. Second, the method assumes a~structural relationship between authorised and unauthorised populations that holds across a~number of communities, in order for the model to be identifiable. Abrupt policy changes (such as the Temporary Protection Directive) can violate this assumption, which would require careful effort to revise any existing model. Third, the approach estimates the \emph{stock} of unauthorised individuals at a~given time, not \emph{gross flows}; year-to-year changes in the stock reflect the net balance of entries, exits, regularisations, and removals. Fourth, the
restriction to non-Schengen nationals excludes EU citizens who may overstay or work without authorisation.

The progression from separate yearly NB-MLE, which may produce volatile estimates sensitive to ad hoc regrouping (Section~\ref{sec-assumptions}), to Poisson PMLE with covariate-varying $\alpha$, baseline offset $\gamma$, and clustered bootstrap provides a~general template for applying the community-anchored models to multi-year administrative data. The framework transfers easily to other countries, just as it has been from Norway in 2006 to Poland in 2024: it requires only aggregated counts that already exist in administrative systems and can be implemented without individual-level data linkage or new data collection. The accompanying R package \texttt{uncounted} provides a~complete
implementation, including estimation, inference, diagnostics, and sensitivity analysis tools.
